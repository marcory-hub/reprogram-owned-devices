# Evaluate: is it any good?

Judge premises, inferences, explanations, and sources.

## Evaluate premises

Ask how plausible each premise is. False premise undermines the argument.

For **testimony:** is the source reliable, sincere, and knowledgeable on the topic? Distinguish real argument from **assuring** (rhetoric without stated reasons).

## Deductive evaluation

**Valid:** if premises are true, conclusion must be true.

**Sound:** valid with all true premises.

**Informal validity test:** can you describe a scenario where premises are true and conclusion false? If yes, invalid (counterexample). Validity is about logical form, not actual truth.

**Invalid forms to flag:** affirming the consequent; denying the antecedent.

## Inductive evaluation

**Strong:** premises make conclusion highly probable. **Weak:** they do not.

Inductive arguments are **defeasible:** new premises can overturn the verdict. Deductive validity is not defeasible in this way.

**Statistical generalization** needs: adequate sample size; non-biased, representative sample. Watch selection bias, self-selected samples, and biased survey wording (**instrument bias**).

**Analogy:** relevant similarities must support the transferred property; check for relevant **disanalogies**. Large enough reference class.

## Abductive / IBE

Infer the hypothesis that best explains the observed facts. Compare competing explanations.

**Seven explanatory virtues** (more met = better):

1. **Explanatoriness:** explains all observed facts
2. **Depth:** does not raise more questions than it answers
3. **Power:** applies across similar contexts, not one case only
4. **Falsifiability:** could be shown false by evidence
5. **Modesty:** does not claim more than needed
6. **Simplicity:** fewer entities or processes (Ockham's razor)
7. **Conservativeness:** does not overturn well-established beliefs without need

## Causal claims

Correlation is necessary but not sufficient for causation. Consider: A causes B, B causes A, common cause C, or accidental correlation.

**False cause / post hoc:** inferring causation from sequence or correlation alone.

Background conditions and joint causes often matter; a stated "cause" may be one factor among many.

## Cognitive bias and heuristics (name when pattern fits)

| Pattern | What to watch for |
| :--- | :--- |
| Confirmation bias | Accepting supportive evidence uncritically; demanding more proof for disconfirming evidence |
| Cherry-picking / Texas sharpshooter | Selecting evidence to fit a fixed conclusion |
| Representativeness heuristic | Judging likelihood by resemblance to a stereotype, ignoring base rates |
| Base rate fallacy | Ignoring how rare the target condition is in the population |
| Conjunction fallacy | Treating a specific conjunction as more probable than a broader category |
| Small numbers / law of small numbers | Small samples swing to extremes; inventing causal stories for noise |
| Regression to the mean | Attributing causation when an extreme value naturally returns toward average |
| Gambler's fallacy | Expecting independent random events to "balance out" in the short run |
| Fundamental attribution error | Explaining others' actions by character, own actions by circumstances |

Describe the distortion when no row matches; do not invent bias names.

## Fallacies (name when pattern clearly fits)

**Ambiguity / equivocation:** key term shifts meaning.

**Circular reasoning / begging the question:** conclusion assumed in premises.

**False dichotomy:** only two options when more exist.

**Composition / division:** illegitimate inference between parts and whole.

**Straw man:** attacking a weaker version of the opposing view.

**Ad hominem:** attacking the person instead of the argument.

**Tu quoque:** deflecting criticism by pointing at the critic's similar fault.

**Genetic fallacy:** rejecting a claim based on its origin alone.

**Appeal to authority:** accepting a claim because an authority said so, without expertise or evidence on point.

**Appeal to consequences:** accepting or rejecting a claim based on desired outcomes, not evidence.

**Slippery slope:** unjustified claim that a small step leads to extreme outcomes (conceptual or causal chain).

**Quoting out of context:** selective quotation that reverses meaning.

**Weak analogy:** superficial similarity used to transfer a property.

**Anecdotal evidence / hasty generalization:** single or tiny sample treated as proof of a general claim.

For other flaws, describe via premise truth, relevance, hidden premises, or inference gaps.

## Fact-check method

1. Extract atomic claims.
2. Label each: OK, overstated, unsupported, [needs verification] or [to be verified]. English only.
3. For drafts with citations, load `science.md`: tier references, label empirical claims, audit statistics.
4. Group: **Fix** / **Soften** / **Check** (what evidence or source type is needed).
5. Do not fabricate citations.

**Decision vs outcome:** a sound inference can fail by chance; flag when text treats luck as proof.

## Source evaluation (when claims cite external information)

| Check | Question |
| :--- | :--- |
| Author | Is there a real, identifiable author or institution? |
| Independence | Do multiple independent sources agree? |
| Bias | Can you separate reported facts from editorial framing? |
| Funding | Who paid for the study or poll? |
| Currency | Is the information still current and in the right context? |
| Plausibility | Does the claim pass basic plausibility before deep research? |
| Cross-check | What do fact-checkers or diverse outlets say? |
| Convenience | Does it fit an ideology too neatly to be credible on its face? |

State what to verify and which source type is needed; do not invent sources.

## Intellectual virtues (evaluation stance)

Apply curiosity, honesty, humility, and charity. Focus on whether the argument is well justified, not on winning a debate.

**Confidence:** high / medium / low + what would change it.
